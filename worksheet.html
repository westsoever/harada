How to use this worksheet

  • Step 0: the goal form. Purpose and past, before any grid.
  • Round 1: find the 8 pillars around the goal.
  • Round 2: 8 actions for each pillar (64 in total).
  • Step 3: start with a few, not all 64.
  • Step 4: decide how to keep it running.

Harada's full sequence: select a meaningful goal; determine its purpose and how it benefits you and others; analyse your past successes and failures; work out 64 tasks; select 10 tasks to get started; write down 10 new routines to change old habits; keep a daily diary; work with a coach or mentor. The grid is only one of his five forms.

Source: Harada & Bodek, The Harada Method: the Spirit of Self-Reliance (2012)

Round 1 · Find the 8 pillars

Collect

Three to five items per prompt. Don't filter yet.

1 · Pre-mortem You're halfway to the deadline and you just gave up. Give five different reasons.

2 · The easy day On a day when working on the goal felt effortless: what was true the night before, that morning, that week?

3 · The worst day A long work day, a short night, travel, illness, the holidays. What would you need in place to still make progress?

4 · Almost there You're close to the deadline and on track. What has changed in your life, beyond the goal itself, that made it possible?

5 · The person What does someone who reaches this goal do, avoid, believe and protect?

6 · People, places and the rest of life Who and what makes it easier or harder? Which part of your life (work, money, home, friends, your head) could quietly derail it?

Cluster

Group everything above into themes. Name each theme in one to three words.

Cut to 8

Each pillar has to pass three tests. At risk: if you neglect it, the goal is genuinely in danger. Actionable: you can do something about it. Distinct: it doesn't overlap with another pillar. Merge near-duplicates. Fewer than 8? Go back to prompts 1 and 3: failure is where hidden pillars show up.

Round 2 · 8 actions per pillar

For each pillar ask: what do I do so that this pillar is never the reason I fall short?

  • Behaviours, not intentions. Together they make a daily, monthly and annual checklist.
  • Each one is a yes/no: a verb, a number and a time.
  • Mix steps you finish once with routines you repeat (daily, weekly, every few weeks).
  • Each daily routine gets a bad-day version: the smallest thing you still do on your worst day, such as 10 minutes instead of an hour.

Source: Jethwani, Dezerv; Robinovitz, Score at the Top

Step 4 · Keep it running

Harada uses a Routine Check Sheet (a daily checklist) and a Daily Diary to reflect on each day. The SAT version adds a weekly diary, a monthly review and a recalibration every 4 weeks. Decide how much of this you will really keep, and where it lives.
